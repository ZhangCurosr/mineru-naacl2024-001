## Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit impressive capabilities but also present risks such as biased content generation and privacy issues. One of the current alignment techniques includes principle-driven integration, but it faces challenges arising from the imprecision of manually crafted rules and inadequate risk perception in models without safety training. To address these, we introduce Guide-Align, a twostage approach. Initially, a safety-trained model identifies potential risks and formulates specific guidelines for various inputs, establish ing a comprehensive library of guidelines and a model for input-guidelines retrieval. Sub sequently, the retrieval model correlates new inputs with relevant guidelines, which guide LLMs in response generation to ensure safe and high-quality outputs, thereby aligning with human values. An additional optional stage in volves fine-tuning a model with well-aligned datasets generated through the process implemented in the second stage. Our method cus tomizes guidelines to accommodate diverse inputs, thereby enhancing the fine-grainedness and comprehensiveness of the guideline li brary. Furthermore, it incorporates safety expertise from a safety-trained LLM through a lightweight retrieval model. We evaluate our approach on three benchmarks, demonstrating significant improvements in LLM security and quality. Notably, our fine-tuned model, Labrador, even at 13 billion parameters, outperforms GPT-3.5-turbo and surpasses GPT-4 in alignment capabilities.<sup>1</sup>

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate impressive capabilities but also pose significant risks, such as generating biased, discriminatory, or privacy-infringing content. Considerable efforts are being made to implement safeguards for LLMs, ensuring their outputs align with human values. Yao et al. (2023) categorize prevalent alignment techniques into three distinct approaches.

The first category, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), improves LLMs’ task-solving abilities and adherence to user instructions through instruction data augmentation (Sanh et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). The second category involves training LLMs using implicit human feedback or comparison signals on pairs of model behaviors, enabling the models to learn human preferences and generate responses accordingly (OpenAI, 2022, 2023; Bai et al., 2022a; Ganguli et al., 2022). The third category integrates LLMs with predefined principles that reflect societal values (Bai et al., 2022b; Solaiman and Dennison, 2021; Sun et al., 2023b; Glaese et al., 2022). One of the most widespread criteria for alignment is ’HHH’, which expects LLMs to be helpful, honest, and harmless (Bai et al., 2022a; Ganguli et al., 2022). Practical applications may involve a synergistic combination of these three types of techniques to optimize the alignment of the model with human values. Nevertheless, the literature reveals two issues with current research efforts:

Limitations of Manually Written Rules Several studies (Sun et al., 2023b; Glaese et al., 2022; Solaiman and Dennison, 2021; Bai et al., 2022b) have used manually crafted rules to align model outputs with human values. Despite some success, these rules have significant limitations: a. Limited Matching Precision: These rules are typically formulated to prioritize generality over specificity to balance applicability and labor costs. Consequently, they offer less precise guidance than those tailored for nuanced, fine-grained matching. b. Limited Comprehensiveness: Predicting all potential deployment scenarios is challenging, making it hard to create rules that effectively cover diverse situations. Moreover, inappropriate rules can introduce noise and unintended side effects, undermining the model’s performance.

Insufficient Risk Perception Models without safety training often lack risk perception, making them ineffective at identifying insecure information within a context. As a result, they are more likely to generate insecure outputs from diverse inputs. Current research approaches, whether they involve prompting AI for self-critique and revision(Bai et al., 2022b) or enabling the model to select and adhere to rules(Sun et al., 2023b), rely on a basic level of security risk awareness of the model. However, models without safety training face challenges in performing these tasks autonomously, particularly without external aids like in-context learning examples.

To address the above issues, we propose a novel method called Guide-Align. This approach autonomously generates a comprehensive library of detailed guidelines tailored to diverse inputs. We also train a retrieval model to associate inputs with relevant guidelines. These resources are then used to guide LLMs during response generation, ensuring safe and high-quality outputs, thereby aligning with human values. As an optional step, we apply our method to open-source input data, creating an alignment dataset. This dataset is used to fine-tune a large model, which we call Labrador.

We foster a more fine-grained match between inputs and guidelines by tailoring specific guidelines for each input. Additionally, we enhance the comprehensiveness of the guideline repository by introducing diversity in inputs while constructing the guideline library. These dual strategies enable our method to overcome limitations associated with manually written rules.

Furthermore, we address the safety awareness deficit in the model lacking safety training by integrating the security expertise derived from a more robust, safety-trained LLM through a lightweight retrieval model.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

• We introduce a novel method named Guide-Align for aligning models with human values.

• We provide a plug-and-play component with a comprehensive and detailed guideline library and a corresponding retrieval model. This component markedly augments the security and quality of LLMs.

• We construct an alignment dataset to provide a valuable resource to facilitate the alignment of models with human values. Furthermore, we utilize this dataset to train a model named Labrador. Despite a parameter scale of 13 billion, Labrador exhibits performance that surpasses GPT-3.5-turbo and exceeds the alignment capabilities of GPT-4 on three benchmarks.

## 2 Our Method: Guide-Align

The Guide-Align framework encompasses two phases: Guideline Library Construction and Retrieval Model Training, followed by Inference. Additionally, we introduce Fine-tuning as an optional phase. The framework of Guide-Align is shown as Figure 1, and we describe each step in detail.

## 2.1 Guideline Library Construction and Retrieval Model Training

In this phase, a safety-trained LLM meticulously analyzes the input data from the training set, determining whether it contains unsafe content or has the potential to prompt AI assistants to generate insecure or inappropriate responses. This step aims to enable the safety-trained LLM to develop targeted rules for safety-relevant input data.

In the event of an affirmative determination by the model (indicated by the term "yes" in the response), the model, contextualizing the safety detection step, is then instructed to formulate guidelines. These guidelines serve to direct AI assistants, ensuring the safety of their responses when confronted with such inputs. Conversely, if the model determines that the input is devoid of insecure content (i.e., indicated by the term "no" in the response), the model is instructed to generate guidelines to enhance the overall quality of AI assistants’ responses when presented with such inputs, without considering the context generated by the safety detection step. An illustrative example of a safetyrelated input and its corresponding set of guidelines is presented in Figure 2.

Let $I = \{ i _ { 1 } , i _ { 2 } , \ldots , i _ { n } \}$ denote the set of inputs, where n represents the size of the training set. For each $i _ { j } \in I \left( j = 1 , 2 , . . . , n \right)$ , a corresponding set of guidelines $G _ { j } = \{ g _ { j , 1 } , g _ { j , 2 } , . . . , g _ { j , k _ { j } } \}$ is derived, where $k _ { j }$ denotes the number of guidelines generated for the jth input. Typically, the guideline generation process, guided by few-shot exemplars, yields $k _ { j }$ values within the range of 5 to 7.

The compiled guideline library (GL) encompass all guides associated with the inputs, forming $\begin{array} { c c l } { G L } & { = } & { \bigcup _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } G _ { j } } \end{array}$ Furthermore, inputguideline pairs are established by pairing each input with its corresponding guidelines. For example, for $i _ { j }$ and its associated guidelines set $G _ { j } =$ $\{ g _ { j , 1 } , g _ { j , 2 } , \dotsc , g _ { j , k _ { j } } \} ,$ k<sub>j</sub> input-guideline pairs are obtained: $( i _ { j } , g _ { j , 1 } ) , ( i _ { j } , g _ { j , 2 } ) , \ldots , ( i _ { j } , g _ { j , k _ { j } } )$ . All resulting input-guideline pairs contribute to the training of a retrieval model designed to match inputs with their associated guides, denoted as g = retrieval\_model(i).

Consequently, the integration of the guideline library and the retrieval model constitutes a plugand-play component that facilitates the generation stage of LLMs.

The focal point of our efforts lies in ensuring the safety of the response generated by LLMs. Simultaneously, we aim to maintain or even enhance the model’s performance in addressing questions unrelated to security. Therefore, our training dataset is curated to encompass both safety-related data and a diverse set of data unrelated to safety. For safety-related inputs, we generated input data containing 8 types of insecure types and 6 types of command attacks using the construction method proposed by Sun et al. (2023a). As for securityindependent inputs, we utilized Alpaca’s training instruction dataset. Detailed statistical information regarding the training set and the guideline library is expounded upon in Section 3.2.

## 2.2 Inference

During the inference stage, the trained retrieval model extracts relevant guidelines from the guideline library for each given input. Given that guidelines are tailored to specific inputs, an influx of input data may result in numerous similar guidelines within the library. To ensure precision in matching, we selectively clean nearly identical guidelines, refraining from stringent deduplication. As a result, the retrieved guidelines may still exhibit substantial similarity. Subsequently, we employ a process based on string fuzzy matching on the retrieved guideline set to impose a more stringent elimination of duplications. The resultant top-k guidelines, post-deduplication, are concatenated with the original input and presented to the LLM to generate safe and high-quality responses.

## 2.3 Fine-tuning (Optional)

Furthermore, we introduce an optional fine-tuning phase. Consistent with the workflow employed during the inference stage, we leverage instructions from an open-source dataset to generate wellaligned responses. However, there is a divergence from the inference stage: to enhance the quality of the generated dataset, we incorporate a small number of examples to assist the model in better adhering to the guidelines. Significantly, these examples are not utilized during the inference stage. The generated responses, in conjunction with the instructions, constitute our newly formed dataset. Subsequently, we utilize this dataset to fine-tune a base model, resulting in a refined model, termed Labrador, which is characterized by its alignment with human values.

## 3 Experiment

## 3.1 Experiment Setup

During the construction of the guideline library, a temperature value of 0.7 is applied to the safetytrained model (referred to as GPT-3.5-turbo in our paper). Throughout the inference and fine-tuning stages, LLMs are decoded with a temperature of 0 to minimize the randomness. We initially retrieve 20 guidelines for each input, and subsequently sample the top 6 after removing duplicates. The deduplication threshold is defined at 0.53. Further details regarding the experimental setup are provided in Section E.

## 3.2 The Statistic Information of Training Set And Guideline Library

During the construction of the guideline library and the training of the retrieval model, we formulate guidelines encompassing 52k security-independent questions drawn from Self-instruct (Wang et al., 2023a) and 100k security-related questions developed following the methodology proposed by Sun et al. (2023a). This aggregation yields a cumulative total of 767k guidelines, and detailed statistical information can be found in Table 1. Subsequently, both the questions and guidelines are utilized as the training, dev, and test set of the retrieval model. However, the guidelines underwent a deduplication process based on string fuzzy matching with a predetermined threshold of 0.75. Following this procedure, the de-duplicated guidelines are incorporated into the guideline repository, resulting in a final repository size of 33k.

## 3.3 Baselines and Labrador

The baseline models utilized in this study include: Vicuna (13b-v1.3)(Chiang et al., 2023), a model devoid of additional safety training; GPT-3.5- turbo(OpenAI, 2022), a model subjected to SFT and RLHF; and GPT-4(OpenAI, 2023), a model that integrates more safety measures such as reinforcement learning with RBRMs (a collection of rule-based reward models).

Labrador represents our well-aligned Language Model (LLM), developed during the fine-tuning phase of the Guide-Align process. Its foundational model is LLaMa-2-13b(Touvron et al., 2023). The training dataset instructions for Labrador are derived from data generated via the Self-Align method, as released by Sun et al. (2023b)<sup>2</sup>, encompassing approximately 28,000 instructions. The output generation adheres to the guidelines established by Vicuna, utilizing two-shot exemplars.

## 3.4.2 HHH\_Alignment

HHH\_Alignment(Bai et al., 2022a; Ganguli et al., 2022) evaluates language models on alignment, pragmatically broken down into the categories of helpfulness, honesty, harmlessness, and other. The benchmark is designed to evaluate model alignment and capabilities without explicitly distinguishing between these facets. We have reframed the task in HHH\_Alignment, treating it as a generative task to assess the model’s performance in producing responses. The refined dataset, devoid of repeated questions, consists of 22 instances in the helpfulness category, 19 in the honesty category, 25 in the harmlessness category, and 37 in the other category.

We conduct four sets of comparison experiments on HHH\_Alignment. Two of these sets aimed to assess the performance of Vicuna and GPT-3.5-turbo with and without the guidelines. The remaining two sets involve the comparative analysis between Labrador and both Vicuna and GPT-3.5-turbo. We task GPT-4 with selecting the superior response from a pair of responses. To mitigate potential biases introduced by the order of options, we systematically reversed the option order, facilitating two evaluations for each question. Table 3 illustrates all four experimental sets’ outcomes.

In the first two experimental series, our observations indicate that adherence to guidelines yields improved results for both Vicuna and GPT-3.5- turbo, surpassing the quality of the original outputs. Nevertheless, the impact of guidelines on these two models is not uniform across instruction types. Specifically, for Vicuna, the guidelines prove more beneficial for the Helpful category, with no discernible assistance for the Honest category. Conversely, GPT-3.5-turbo exhibits the opposite pattern. We posit that this inconsistency may be attributed to inherent dissimilarities in the models capacities across different types of questions, as well as their divergent abilities to adhere to varied types of guidelines. This aspect will be subject to further investigation in our subsequent research endeavors.

Additionally, the subsequent two sets of experimental results are noteworthy for distinctly demonstrating Labrador’s consistent outperformance over Vicuna and GPT-3.5-turbo across various categories. This underscores Labrador’s remarkable ability to align with human values.

## 3.6.2 Identifying Risks through Retrieval Model

We conceptualize the guideline library, retrieval model, and LLM for generating responses as an integrated AI system. Our designed AI system encapsulates GPT-3.5-turbo’s risk awareness by utilizing the retrieval model. It addresses the challenge posed by LLMs lacking safety training, which results in insufficient risk perception, hindering their ability to select appropriate rules autonomously.

To verify the system’s risk identification capabilities, we conduct a series of comparative experiments on the "do\_not\_answer" dataset. Specifically, we employ the retrieval model to retrieve guidelines for each input. Success is defined as the system correctly identifying safety-related guidelines within the top 3 retrieved. In contrast, we task the Vicuna with detecting the harmfulness of each input under both zero-shot and 5-shot conditions. The respective risk identification accuracies for the system, the Vicuna with zero-shot and with 5-shot are 94.7%, 39.0%, and 42.4%. Consequently, we infer that the retrieval model effectively aligns safety-related rules with unsafe inputs, thereby augmenting the overall system’s risk awareness. Notably, this risk awareness remains consistent and unaffected by variations in the performance of LLMs.

## 4 Related Work

When aligning through Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), models are trained with human-valued data (Solaiman and Dennison, 2021). Various strategies, such as Constitutional-AI(Bai et al., 2022b) and Self-Align (Sun et al., 2023b), are designed to employ LLMs to generate instruction data for alignment. Despite its benefits, SFT struggles with generalization and lacks negative feedback. Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) has been introduced to overcome these issues, which uses a reward model trained on model-generated responses (OpenAI, 2022, 2023; Bai et al., 2022a; Ganguli et al., 2022; Ouyang et al., 2022). However, RLHF is costly, unstable, and sensitive to hyperparameters.

An alternative approach is incorporating alignment goals into prompts without modifying the model structure or parameters. Bai et al. (2022b) instructs the model to criticize and revise its noncompliant responses through prompts, aiming for a final safe response. Similarly, Sun et al. (2023b) formulates 16 generic rules, which are adaptively added to prompts and mandated for adherence by the model. However, manually written rules suffer from limited precision and comprehensiveness, and LLMs’ self-critique or automatic match rules necessitate risk awareness, which depends on the base model’s performance. Our approach addresses these limitations by automatically generating a comprehensive and detailed guideline library using a safety-trained model. In addition, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)(Lewis et al., 2020) has proven efficacy in mitigating issues such as model hallucinations(Gao et al., 2023) and knowledge staleness through knowledge retrieval. In contrast, we employ a trained retrieval model for guideline matching, ensuring the safety of the responses and stability irrespective of the base model’s performance.

## 5 Conclusion

We propose Guide-Align, a guideline-oriented method to augment the safety and quality of LLMs. We construct a comprehensive and detailed guideline library complemented by a retrieval model that matches inputs with appropriate guidelines. These elements constitute a plug-and-play module that enables various LLMs to achieve alignment with minimal expenditure. Experiments across diverse datasets consistently illustrate that our methodology bolsters model safety while preserving or enhancing performance in other dimensions. Furthermore, we curate an alignment dataset and employ it to fine-tune a secure and dependable model, denoted as Labrador.

## Limitations

Here, we discuss two limitations of our method:

Relying on a Safety-Trained LLM Our approach necessitates reliance on a robust, safetytrained LLM in the initial phase to generate guidelines. Consequently, the quality of the guidelines is contingent upon this model’s capabilities. Furthermore, a diverse range of inputs is employed to ensure the comprehensiveness of guidelines, albeit with associated cost implications. Therefore, despite the desirability of using GPT-4, we have chosen to employ GPT-3.5-turbo.

Constrained Cross-Linguistic Applicability Our methodology involves employing a single language retrieval model for extracting guidelines from a library. However, considering that the input may likely encompass multiple languages, we recognize the necessity to establish a multilingual guidelines library and train a cross-language retrieval model in the future.

## Broader Impact

Our research endeavors to enhance the safety and practicality of models without compromising their performance. Simultaneously, we aspire to encourage researchers to delve into the alignment of values concerning LLMs. The alignment dataset and model we publish serve as a valuable resource, fostering broader engagement within the research community and empowering researchers to explore and advance the field of value alignment.

However, it is crucial to acknowledge and mitigate potential negative impacts associated with our work. A notable concern is the risk of misuse, wherein, under the worst-case scenario, this guide-based alignment approach could be altered to a poisoning method based on malicious guidance. This emphasizes the need for sustained vigilance and ethical oversight throughout the development and deployment of AI technologies.

Furthermore, the openness of the guideline library and alignment datasets introduces the possibility of reinforcing biases. Unforeseen biases within the open-source guideline library or alignment datasets could inadvertently perpetuate unintended biases in AI systems. The presence of biased information in these resources poses a significant ethical challenge and underscores the necessity for continuous scrutiny, transparency, and efforts to mitigate unintended consequences.

## D Additional Related Work

RedTeaming Data To enhance model security, Bai et al. (2022a); Ganguli et al. (2022) employed manual red-team testing, wherein red-team members craft potentially risky inputs to elicit insecure behavior in their models. Despite its effectiveness, the manual creation of malicious prompts and corresponding answers necessitates substantial labor and time investments. Consequently, an increasingly favored approach today involves the automated generation of red-team data, characterized by two primary methodologies: prompt injection and adversarial model training. In the former, existing prompts transform jailbreak prompts(Yu et al., 2023; Perez and Ribeiro, 2022; Abdelnabi et al., 2023), while the latter involves training an adversarial model to produce new malicious inputs continually. For instance, Ge et al. (2023) employed a pre-curated seed dataset of over 2,400 instances to train an adversarial model. This involved supplying a malicious prompt as input and obtaining a prompt of the same violation category or attack style as output. The resultant model was subsequently employed to target a given model, with iterative refinements based on prompts from successful attacks. In our work, we refer to the approach of (Sun et al., 2023a) to augment our security-related guidelines by instructing the model to generate red team data.

Evaluation of Alignment Currently, four primary approaches exist for assessing model alignment. First, benchmark datasets with labeled answers such as Big-bench(Srivastava et al., 2022), Truthful-QA(Lin et al., 2022), SafetyBench(Zhang et al., 2023), and do\_not\_answer(Wang et al., 2023b) are utilized. Evaluations encompass generative, discriminative, and multiple-choice tasks tailored to the respective benchmark categories. Second, the assessment may be conducted manually or by experts, involving a comparison between the output of the target model and a reference answer, resulting in metrics such as win rate or Elo score(Askell et al., 2021). Third, an automated dialog bot arena can be established due to the resource-intensive nature of manual evaluations. Here, a high-capacity LLM (e.g., GPT-4) serves as a judge(Wang et al., 2023b; Chiang et al., 2023), evaluating responses of Large Language Models (LLMs) by comparing them across various aspects. Lastly, a reward model or a classifier can be trained. The scores returned by the reward model and the labels from the classifier serve as effective evaluation metrics(Bai et al., 2022a; Rafailov et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2023). In our experiments, the evaluator comprises the trained classifier and GPT-4.
